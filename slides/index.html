Good prototypes are close to their data points

k-means minimizes one objective

k-means minimizes $J$ by two alternating updates

Goal Each prototype should stand for the data points of its group.

We need one number for how well they do, and prototypes that make it small.

We split the data points into $C$ clusters (here $C=3$).
The membership $u_{ci}$ says which cluster data point $i$ is in, so choosing $u$ is the clustering.

Minimize $J$ in turns: assign with the prototypes fixed, then average.

$$J=\underbrace{\class{t-s}{\sum_i\sum_c}}_{\substack{\text{all data points}\\\text{and clusters}}}\;\underbrace{\class{t-u}{u_{ci}}}_{\substack{1\text{ if }i\text{ is in }c\text{,}\\\text{else }0}}\;\underbrace{\class{t-d}{\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2}}_{\substack{\text{squared}\\\text{distance}}}$$
$$\underbrace{\class{t-ua}{u_{ci}=1\ \text{for}\ c=\mathop{\arg\min}_k\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_k\|^2}}_{\text{fix }\bm{b}\text{: the nearest prototype}}$$ $$\underbrace{\class{t-bm}{\bm{b}_c=\frac{\sum_i u_{ci}\bm{x}_i}{\sum_i u_{ci}}}}_{\text{fix }u\text{: the mean}}$$

k-means is a clustering method that represents each cluster by the mean of its data points.

$J$ measures the fit: the squared distance from each data point to its prototype, summed over all data points.$\bm{x}_i$: data point $i$; $\bm{b}_c$: the prototype of cluster $c$.

Each update minimizes $J$, so $J$ never increases.

MacQueen, 1967

k-means splits clusters with a crisp boundary

FCM expresses the boundary by degrees

Problem k-means forces a data point halfway between two clusters to pick one.

So we let membership be a degree in $[0,1]$.

The same $J$ as k-means, but membership is a degree, softened by the power $m$.

Both updates minimize $J$: degrees from distances, prototypes as weighted means.

$$J=\sum_i\sum_c \underbrace{\class{t-u}{u_{ci}^{\,m}}}_{\text{degree, to the power }m}\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2$$
$$\underbrace{u_{ci}=\frac{\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^{-2/(m-1)}}{\sum_k\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_k\|^{-2/(m-1)}}}_{\text{nearer, larger; summing to 1}}$$ $$\underbrace{\bm{b}_c=\frac{\sum_i u_{ci}^{\,m}\,\bm{x}_i}{\sum_i u_{ci}^{\,m}}}_{\text{weighted mean}}$$

Idea The nearer a prototype, the larger the degree; each data point’s degrees add up to 1.

FCM (fuzzy c-means) is a clustering method that gives each data point a degree of membership in every cluster.

$u_{ci}\in[0,1]$, summing to 1 over the clusters.$m>1$ is the fuzzifier: the larger $m$, the softer the degrees.

$J$ never increases.FCM keeps the objective and the two updates of k-means, with degrees in place of 0 or 1.

In rough set theory, neighborhoods give rise to uncertainty

Rough sets judge each data point by its neighborhood

Insight A data point is seen only through its neighbors: if they are in different clusters, its cluster is uncertain.

So we judge each data point by its neighborhood, not by distance.

The neighborhood of a data point: every data point within a radius $\delta$, itself included.

$$\underbrace{\mathcal{N}_i}_{\text{the neighborhood of }i}=\{\,j:\ \|\bm{x}_i-\bm{x}_j\|\le \delta\,\}$$

Idea If all neighbors of a data point are in a cluster, the data point is certainly in it; if only some are, possibly.

As $\delta$ shrinks, $\mathcal{N}_i$ becomes pure and $i$ certain.

Pawlak, 1982

Rough membership grades a data point by its neighbors

Rough membership is the share of a data point's neighbors that are in the cluster.

All neighbors in the cluster: certain; neighbors in both: possibly in either.

$$\mu_{ci}=\underbrace{\frac{|\mathcal{N}_i\cap X_c|}{|\mathcal{N}_i|}}_{\text{share of cluster }c\text{ among the neighbors}}$$
$$\underbrace{\mu_{ci}=1}_{\text{certainly in }c}$$
$$\underbrace{0<\mu_{ci}<1}_{\text{possibly in }c\text{ (boundary)}}$$

$X_c$: the data points in cluster $c$ (labels from k-means).

$\mu_{ci}$ is a neighborhood-induced fuzzy membership.We can extract certain and uncertain regions by $\mu_{ci}$.

RMCM2 puts rough membership into one objective

Ours

We proposed rough-membership-based rough clustering (RMCM2).
RMCM2 minimizes the k-means objective with rough membership in place of the crisp assignment.

The update rules of the labels and the prototypes are derived by minimizing $J$.
Labels go by the smaller cost $q$; prototypes are $\mu$-weighted means.

$$J=\sum_c\sum_i \underbrace{\class{t-mu}{\mu_{ci}}}_{\substack{\text{rough membership,}\\\text{in place of }u}}\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2$$
$$q_{cj}=\underbrace{\sum_i P_{ij}\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2}_{\substack{\bm{b}_c\text{'s error over the data points}\\\text{with }j\text{ in their neighborhoods}}}$$ $$\underbrace{\bm{b}_c=\frac{\sum_i \mu_{ci}\,\bm{x}_i}{\sum_i \mu_{ci}}}_{\mu\text{-weighted mean}}$$

$P_{ij}=1/|\mathcal{N}_i|$ if $j\in\mathcal{N}_i$, else 0.Each update minimizes $J$, so $J$ never increases.

Ubukata, Notsu, Honda, Inf. Sci., 2021

The SOM orders prototypes on a lattice

1. An input $\bm{x}$ comes in.
2. Its nearest prototype is found: the winner $w$.
3. The winner and the nodes near it in the latent space move toward $\bm{x}$ together.

$$\underbrace{\bm{b}_c}_{\text{node }c}\leftarrow\bm{b}_c+\underbrace{\alpha}_{\text{rate}}\,\underbrace{h(c,w)}_{\substack{w\text{: the winner,}\\\text{nearest to }\bm{x}}}\,\underbrace{(\bm{x}-\bm{b}_c)}_{\substack{\text{toward}\\\text{the input}}}$$ $$\underbrace{h(c,w)=e^{-\|\bm{r}_c-\bm{r}_w\|^2/2\sigma^2}}_{\substack{\text{pulls the winner's neighbors too}\\\bm{r}_c\text{: node }c\text{ on the lattice; }\sigma\text{: reach}}}$$

In batch SOM, each node moves to a weighted mean of all data points.

$$\bm{b}_c=\frac{\sum_i h(c,w_i)\,\bm{x}_i}{\sum_i h(c,w_i)}$$ $$\underbrace{w_i=\mathop{\arg\min}_k\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_k\|^2}_{\text{the winner of data point }i}$$

The shading on the right is $h(c,w)$: largest at the winner, less for its lattice neighbors.

On the lattice, a data point can only sit on its winner node.→ Can each data point have its own position on the lattice?

SOM-OLP adds one variable: the latent position

SOM-OLP minimizes one objective

SOM-OLP minimizes $J$ in closed form, one block at a time

Given The SOM's prototypes $\bm{b}$ and memberships $u$ (soft winners), plus a new variable: each data point's latent position $\bm{v}_i$.

Find all three by minimizing one objective, so that no update makes it worse.

Ours

One objective: data fit, lattice distance of each position $\bm{v}$, entropy of $u$.

Fix two blocks of variables and minimize over the third, in closed form.

$$J=\sum_{i,c}u_{ci}\bigl(\underbrace{\class{t-fit}{\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2}}_{\text{data fit}}+\underbrace{\class{t-lat}{\gamma\|\bm{v}_i-\bm{r}_c\|^2}}_{\text{lattice distance}}+\underbrace{\class{t-ent}{\lambda\ln u_{ci}}}_{\text{entropy}}\bigr)$$
$$\underbrace{\class{t-bu}{u_{ci}\propto \exp(-q_{ci}/\lambda)}}_{\text{softmax}}$$ $$\underbrace{\class{t-bv}{\bm{v}_i=\sum_c u_{ci}\,\bm{r}_c}}_{\text{mean of nodes}}$$ $$\underbrace{\class{t-bb}{\bm{b}_c=\frac{\sum_i u_{ci}\bm{x}_i}{\sum_i u_{ci}}}}_{\text{mean of data}}$$

Memberships $u$ link the data space and the latent space.

$\bm{r}_c$: node $c$'s place on the lattice; $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ weigh the lattice distance and the entropy.$q_{ci}$: the data fit plus the lattice distance, the cost of node $c$ for data point $i$.

No block increased $J$ here.$O(nC)$ per iteration ($n$ data points, $C$ nodes); Heskes's energy: $O(nC^2)$.

Ubukata, Notsu, Honda, IJCNN 2026

Ubukata, Notsu, Honda, IJCNN 2026 · Heskes, 1999

Supplement: Heskes's SOM minimizes one energy

Problem SOM updates are rules that decrease no known function.

So we want SOM learning to minimize one energy, as k-means does.

One energy for all the errors, each winner chosen to make its own term smallest.

$$E=\sum_i\sum_c \underbrace{h(c,w_i)}_{\text{near the winner}}\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_c\|^2$$ $$\underbrace{w_i=\mathop{\arg\min}_c\sum_k h(c,k)\,\|\bm{x}_i-\bm{b}_k\|^2}_{\text{the winner minimizes its term of }E}$$

Idea Choose each winner by how well its whole lattice neighborhood fits the data point: then one energy is minimized.

Batch SOM on the surface: its updates are rules, with no objective.

A data point $\bm{x}$ on the surface.The lattice: its nearest node and its winner.

On the same surface and lattice, the energy-based winner can differ from the nearest node: for 363 of 800 data points (with $h$ of width $\sigma$ = 1).

Every candidate's term sums over all nodes: $O(nC^2)$ work per iteration.

Heskes, 1999
